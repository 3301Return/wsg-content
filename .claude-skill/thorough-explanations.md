# Thorough Explanations

Every WSG article has a core: the thing the reader came to learn how to do. Answer this interview question. Build a strong resume. Structure a STAR answer. Get through the case. This skill makes sure **the core is explained deeply and everything else stays short.** Drafts fail in both directions, and this skill catches both.

## The two failures (October 14, 2026)

The founder: "there are times where the articles don't explain enough, let's say an interview question and explaining the steps and what it means. In other times the issue is too much explanation that isn't relevant to the paper. For example, writing about the history of resumes or explaining how banks score interviews, research behind structured interviews. The focus should be either how to make a strong resume, how to highlight your points, knowing what you put in your resume."

**1. Too little on the core.** The draft names the steps of the thing the reader has to do but doesn't walk them, or gives an example answer without saying what each part is doing. Signs:

- An interview question with a sample answer and no explanation of what the question is asking for, how to build the answer step by step, or what each part of the sample does.
- A how-to that lists steps as labels ("quantify your bullets") without showing how to do it, with a before and after.
- A process named but not walked. "Run the comps, then triangulate."
- A worked example that names the inputs and the result but skips the arithmetic.

**2. Too much off the core.** The draft spends its words on the system around the task instead of the task. Signs:

- History and origin stories (the history of resumes, who invented a method).
- How firms score, screen or decide, told from the firm's side (scoring rubrics, how banks evaluate interviews).
- Research about the process (studies on structured interviews, validity figures, application-volume statistics).
- Levels and detail above the reader's seat, examples out of proportion, and the same lesson repeated in several formats.

The test for any paragraph: **does it help the reader do the core thing better?** Steps, what each step means, how to do it well, what a strong version looks like, the common mistakes and their fixes: yes, explain fully. How the system works, where it came from, what studies show: two lines at most, usually none.

## Where the depth comes from: research how to do it well

The explanations of the core should come from research, not from general knowledge. Before drafting, research how the best sources teach the core task: career-office guides (MIT, Harvard, Wharton and the like), the firms' own candidate pages, established prep sites (Wall Street Prep, Mergers & Inquisitions, Management Consulted, Hacking the Case Interview), practitioners' write-ups. Collect what they agree on about **how to do it**: the steps, the structure, what a strong version contains, the mistakes they see, worked examples. Then reuse that material, in WSG's own plain words and with credit where a source is named, as the article's explanations.

Research about the system (how firms score, the history of a method, studies on interview validity) is still worth reading to get facts right, but it stays in the notes. The research that goes into the article is the research on doing the thing well.

## How deep the core goes

For each core item, the explanation is done when the reader could do it themselves:

1. **What it is and what it's asking for**, in plain words. For an interview question: what the interviewer is trying to learn from it, in one or two sentences, phrased as what to show ("they want to hear how you handled the disagreement, not who was right").
2. **The steps**, each as its own sentence or short paragraph, in order.
3. **What each step means and how to do it well**, with the detail the research gave you.
4. **One worked example** (a sample answer, a before-and-after bullet, a case walk), and where it isn't obvious, a short note on what each part of the example is doing.
5. **The common mistakes, as fixes**, if they change what the reader does.

Then stop. One example per mechanism, sized to the article. Don't explain the same step again in a table, a recap or a say/don't block.

If there's no editorial plan, write the three-line version first (reader, the core thing they came to do, what's background) and apply the same split.

**Complete is not the same as long.** A thorough article goes deep once on the core and moves on. Length follows the question (SKILL.md): focused pieces about 2,500 to 3,500 words, in-depth guides up to about 6,000.

## When this runs

- Automatically on every WSG article: after the plain-language pass, before the prose-editing pass. Again on every revision. It checks the core for missing depth and everything else for overexplanation.
- On request for any other draft.
- **The fail-safe (mandatory on WSG articles):** after the article is delivered, append the Thoroughness Report (format below).

## More signs of a thin core: jumpy and over-condensed explanations

**Jumps (missing steps)**

- A sentence goes from cause to effect with no mechanism. "Rising rates hurt LBO returns." How?
- A process is named but not walked. "Run the comps, then triangulate."
- A "why" is asserted, not shown. "Banks prefer sophomores who network early."
- A term of art is used as if it were the explanation. "Because of the waterfall."
- An H2 sub-question answered in one sentence when it needed three or four.
- A heading promises a how-to and the body delivers a what.

**Over-condensation (steps present but crushed)**

- One sentence carrying three ideas joined by commas or "and."
- A bolded claim doing the whole job of a paragraph, with no support under it.
- A numbered list where each item is a label, not an explanation.
- A worked example that names the inputs and the result but skips the arithmetic.
- A comparison that gives the verdict without the two or three axes it was decided on.

## More signs of off-core bloat

These add length without helping the reader do the core thing.

- An explanation of how the system works (scoring rubrics, research validity figures, a firm's process from its side) when the reader came to learn what to do.
- Detail above the reader's level (a full senior pay ladder in an entry-level guide).
- A worked example that runs several pages in an article that isn't about that example.
- The same lesson shown in an example, explained after it, mapped in a table and repeated in a say/don't block.
- The same mechanism worked twice with different numbers.
- A table and a paragraph saying the same thing.
- A section that ends by restating what the section said.
- An "honest read," "the trade-off" or "what this means for you" section that repeats earlier sections.
- Background the reader didn't come for (a firm's founding story at full length, every deal of the year).
- A closing section that summarizes the article.

## The fix, in order

For each thin passage in the core:

1. **Name the question the passage is really answering.** One line, for yourself.
2. **List the steps a reader needs** to get from the question to the answer. Usually three to six. If you can't list them, research how good sources teach it before expanding.
3. **Write each step as its own sentence or short paragraph.** One idea per sentence for anything technical. One concrete example with plausible numbers when quantities are involved.
4. **Check the chain.** At every sentence: could the reader have predicted this from the one before? If there's a gap, add the bridge.
5. **Keep the bolded claim**, and make sure the text under it now earns it.

Then **pay for it.** For every passage you expand, look for bloat (list above) in the same section or elsewhere and cut it. The goal is a focused article that answers the question within the plan's budget.

Expanding is not padding. Padding restates; expanding adds a step, a reason, a number, or an example that wasn't there.

## What this skill protects

- The WSG skim narrative: bolded sentences stay self-contained full sentences, and the bold-only read still tells the argument.
- Intros stay short (`intros.md`). This skill works on the body only.
- Listicle items keep their bullet structure. Expansion goes into the description and the takeaway, not into a wall of prose per item.
- Prose editing still runs afterward (`prose-editing.md`), including rule 16, the length cut. It cuts repetition and side material; it never cuts a step this pass added.

## The fail-safe: Thoroughness Report

After delivering any WSG article, append this report in the chat after the file. Keep it short.

```
Thoroughness Report
- Core depth added: {N} passages
  - {section or H2}: {what was thin and what was added, and the source it came from}
- Off-core passages cut or shortened: {N}
  - {section}: {what was cut back}
- Cut for length: {N} passages
  - {section}: {what was cut}
- Still thin (needs a source, a number, or the user's input): {N}
  - {section}: {what's missing and why it couldn't be filled}
- Plain-language rewrites: {N} paragraphs (terms explained at first use: {term, term, term})
- Word count: {final} (plan budget: {focused or in-depth, range}; about {pages} pages before images)
- Over budget? {no | yes, because ...}
```

Post it with zeros if nothing was expanded or cut. A missing report means the check didn't run.

"Still thin" entries are the important ones: passages that can't be fixed without information Claude doesn't have (a real deadline, a comp number). Never paper over a thin spot with filler. Flag it and let the user decide.
