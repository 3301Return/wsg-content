# WSG Intros

Updated October 8, 2026 from Stephen's reference articles and notes. This replaces the October 7 version. The September rules (rotating hooks, opening stories, the Stephen Turban credential intro) stay retired.

## The rule

**Answer the question first.** The first sentence says what the topic is, or answers the question in the headline, in plain words. Then one or two facts the reader needs. Then one line on what the article covers. Then the article starts.

No narrative openings. No scene, no story, no "most candidates walk in expecting...," no buildup toward the point. Stephen's note: be quick and snappy, then get to the value.

**Length:** two to five sentences, one or two short paragraphs. A listicle can be shorter: one or two sentences, then the list.

## The pattern

1. **Sentence one: what it is, or the direct answer.** Use the target keyword. Say it the way a student would explain it to a friend.
2. **Sentence two: the one fact that matters most, bolded as a full sentence.** A real number or a concrete detail (how long it is, how many people get in, what it leads to). Verified and VERIFY-tagged if it can move.
3. **Optional: one more line of facts.** Format, length, timing, the main options.
4. **Last sentence: what the article covers.** "In this article, I lay out..." or "Here's what each one is, who it's for, and how to apply." Light first person is fine here (see Voice below).

## The reference openings (Stephen's picks, October 2026)

These are the openings to calibrate on. They're from published WSG articles that Stephen marked as good.

**Defines the topic in sentence one** (How to Beat McKinsey Solve):

> McKinsey Solve is the gamified pre-interview assessment every McKinsey candidate has to pass before getting a case interview. Roughly 60-75 minutes long, two mini-games, and a pass/fail score that McKinsey has never published the exact threshold for.

**Bolded full sentence with a number, right after the opener** (Top 10 Sophomore Insights Programs for IB):

> Insight programs are one of the best ways into Bulge Bracket and Elite Boutique investment banking. **Students who get into one of these programs are roughly 5 to 10 times more likely to convert into a Summer Analyst offer than students who skip them and apply through the open online portal.**

Stephen's note: the bolded line is a full sentence, not a phrase.

**Listicle intro that gets straight to the value** (10 Investment Banking Internships to Apply To in 2027):

> If you're applying to Investment Banking Summer Analyst programs in 2027, you're competing for Summer 2028 seats.

**Plain first-person framing** (Is Wall Street Prep Worth It?):

> I get asked about Wall Street Prep a lot. Students want to know if it's worth the money, whether it'll actually help them get an offer, and whether there's something better they should be using instead.

## Bad openings and their fixes (Stephen's examples)

**Bad: narrative opener that never answers the question** (The Ultimate Guide to the BCG Casey Chatbot Interview):

> Most candidates walk into the BCG Casey chatbot interview expecting a casual screening step and walk out rattled. There is no interviewer to read, no chance to ask a clarifying question, and a clock that never stops... That is the part that catches people. Casey is not a personality quiz dressed up as a game.

Stephen: it doesn't address the question, it's generic, and it's weirdly opinionated about what people get wrong without showing evidence.

**Fix: start with what it is.**

> The BCG Casey chatbot interview is the first-round interview for BCG candidates before you go to a case round. [Then: how long it is, what it tests, what the article covers.]

**Bad: clever line plus a crammed stat** (Top 10 Consulting Exit Opportunities):

> Consulting's best-kept secret is that the job is a lobby, not a house. A LinkedIn analysis of 1,644 people who left MBB (McKinsey & Company, Boston Consulting Group (BCG), and Bain & Company) in late 2025 found them scattering across finance, software, retail, startups, and, most of all, other consulting firms, mostly at private companies, mostly within three years of joining.

Stephen: "this is terrible."

**Fix: one plain fact, the question the reader has, the short answer, then what the article covers.**

> The average tenure for consultants is about two years. So where do consultants go after? Most end up in one of four places: private equity, tech, large companies, or their own startup.
>
> In this article, I lay out the main paths people take after consulting.

(Verify the tenure figure before using it; the shape is the point.)

## What these show

- The good openings answer "what is this?" or "what do I get?" in the first sentence. The bad ones make the reader wait.
- The good openings state facts. The bad ones state opinions about the reader ("most candidates," "the part that catches people") with no evidence.
- The good openings use one number in one clean sentence. The bad one stacks a stat, a parenthetical list of firm names and three "mostly" clauses into one sentence.
- The good openings sound like someone talking. "A lobby, not a house," "a clock that never stops" and "dressed up as a game" sound written.

## Voice

Light first person is allowed in the intro (October 8, 2026): "I get asked about X a lot," "In this article, I lay out...," "Here's the list I'd send a sophomore." "We" for WSG and "you" for the reader are also fine.

Still out: credentials and bio ("I went to Harvard," "as a former McKinsey consultant," "I'm the founder of WSG"), personal stories and memories, composite students, and any author introduction.

## What never goes in an intro

- **No narrative openings.** No scene, story, anecdote, "picture this," or "most candidates walk in..."
- **No opinions about the reader without evidence.** "Most students get this wrong," "that's the part that catches people," "students underestimate X."
- **No negatives about the writer, WSG or the article.** "I'm not a banker," "this guide won't cover X."
- **No clever lines.** Metaphors, "X is not Y, it's Z," "dressed up as," "best-kept secret," "here's the thing." Every sentence passes the say-it-out-loud test.
- **No crammed sentences.** One fact per sentence. Long firm-name expansions go in the body.
- **No buildup.** No "Let's dive in," "In today's market," "The truth is."

## Checklist

- First sentence says what the topic is or answers the headline's question, with the target keyword.
- A bolded full sentence with a concrete fact in the first two or three sentences (when the article has a fact worth bolding).
- Last sentence says what the article covers.
- Two to five sentences total; a listicle can be one or two.
- No story, scene, or opinion about what readers get wrong.
- First person, if used, is framing only. No bio, no credentials, no memories.
- No em dashes. Periods, commas, colons.
