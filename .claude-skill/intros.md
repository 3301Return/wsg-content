# WSG Intros

The first three paragraphs decide whether a sophomore keeps reading. This skill covers what goes in them and what stays out. Keep it simple: one hook, one Stephen intro, one promise of value. Then the article starts.

## The three parts of every opening

**1. A hook that draws attention.** Pick one type per article and rotate across articles. Never the same type or the same shape two articles in a row.

- **Decision moment.** Someone holding two options and calling for advice. "A sophomore I worked with at WSG last cycle, call him D, had a Goldman IB offer in one hand and a Morgan Stanley equity research offer in the other and called me asking which to take."
- **Scene.** A specific moment, described plainly. An inbox on the morning applications open. A superday waiting room. A 2 a.m. text before a first-round.
- **Friend, roommate, or family story.** Not every story has to be a student Stephen advised. A college roommate who went to Evercore, a friend's younger brother deciding on a sophomore program, a cousin who picked consulting over banking.
- **Stat with a bite.** One real number the reader didn't know, followed by why it matters to them. Verify it and tag it if it moves.
- **Direct claim.** A short, opinionated sentence the reader will want to argue with. "Most undergrads default to investment banking because it's louder."
- **Question the reader is already asking.** Phrased the way they'd type it, then answered in the article.
- **First-call quote.** Something a student actually said on a first call, in quotes, then the reality behind it.

Two to three short paragraphs total. Keyword in the first 100 words. No "in this guide we will."

**2. The Stephen Turban intro.** Always present, within the first three paragraphs. Placement and wording vary: before the hook, after it, or woven into the story. Examples of the range:

- One line: "I'm Stephen Turban, founder of WSG, and I've watched this exact choice play out dozens of times."
- Woven: "...called me asking which to take. I run WSG, and I get a version of this call every spring."
- Credential-led: "My name is Stephen Turban. I went to Harvard, went through McKinsey's on-cycle recruiting, and now run WallStreetGuide, where I advise undergrads through the IB and consulting pipelines."

Use only the credentials in `writers/stephen-turban.md` (Harvard College, McKinsey, Lumiere co-founder, WSG founder). Rotate which ones appear; not every intro needs the full list. McKinsey is the natural credential for consulting, case prep, and any on-cycle process article; WSG for anything recruiting-process. Never invent biography, never "as an IB analyst," never a firm that isn't on the profile. If a new credential belongs in the rotation, it goes on the profile first.

**3. The promise.** One or two sentences on what the reader leaves with, tied back to the hook. "This guide breaks down the comparison the way I broke it down with D, so you can pick on what you actually want from your week."

## Story rules

**WSG never looks bad in its own stories.** A student Stephen coached who then loses out makes WSG look ineffective. Setbacks happen *before* someone finds WSG ("came to WSG a week after her Bain rejection"), or they happen to a roommate, friend, or someone Stephen watched from the outside. Students advised by WSG win: offers, good reroutes, sound decisions between two offers.

**No fake-precise effort counts.** "Ran 47 practice cases with one partner," "two hundred flashcards," "40 hours of Moyer" read as invented. Nobody does 47 cases in a row with one person. Use roundable, plausible language: "a semester of cases," "a fall working through Moyer," "more mock interviews than she wanted to." Real outcome numbers stay (a 3.6 GPA, a $9,000 sponsorship close, a signing bonus).

**Composite people get single-letter names.** D, M, R. Never a full fake name, never a real student's name.

**Vary the story, not just the type.** Different firms, different years, different outcomes, different tension. If the last article opened on a two-offer decision, this one shouldn't.

## Wording rules: the "say it out loud" test

Every sentence in the opening (and, through the prose-editing pass, the whole article) has to pass one test: **would Stephen say this sentence out loud to a student across a table?** If not, write the plain version.

Cut on sight, and anything in their family:

| Cut this | Why |
| --- | --- |
| "That's the headline." | Nobody talks like this. |
| "the caveats the recruiting brochures leave out" | Nobody hands out brochures. Odd object, odd rhythm. |
| "before counting retirement money" | Unclear and strange. Say "before the 401(k) match" or cut it. |
| "life happens in deposits," "which kind of tired you want to be," "the smallest interesting number" | Copywriter lines. They sound written, not said. |
| "the interview's real center of gravity," "reads as a future engagement summary," "pressure-tested for your fingerprint" | Metaphor stacked on metaphor. Say the plain thing. |
| "in every way that counts," "an embarrassment of material," "no faking automatic," "choosing on noise," "holdouts need dragging," "case clock," "cheap homework," "mental math under a clock" | Off-register, compressed, or cute. Plain version every time. |
| "Rent is not national." | A fragment pretending to be an insight. Say what you mean. |

The pattern behind the list: a phrase that sounds clever on the page but that no one would say in conversation. Concrete, plain, spoken-sounding wording always wins. When a sentence feels strained, fix it with a specific fact, not a safer cliché (see prose-editing rule 4).

## Checklist before the draft leaves the intro

- One hook, one type, different from the last article's.
- Stephen intro present in the first three paragraphs, credentials from the profile only.
- Promise of value tied to the hook.
- Keyword in the first 100 words.
- Two to three short paragraphs. No four-paragraph buildup.
- No story where WSG advised someone who then lost.
- No effort counts that sound invented.
- Every sentence passes "would Stephen say this out loud."
- No em dashes. Periods, commas, colons.

## Pairing

Runs on the draft before `plain-language` and `thorough-explanations` (those expand the body, not the intro; intros stay tight). The "say it out loud" test and the cut list are mirrored in the wsg-article `prose-editing.md` module so they apply to the whole article, not just the opening.
