# Case Interview Frameworks Library

The case interview is the central screening mechanism for MBB, tier-2, Big 4 strategy, and most boutiques. WSG articles on case interviews must use the framework vocabulary correctly. This file is the canonical framework library. It is intentionally opinionated — the framework canon is well-established in consulting prep, and any article that gets the vocabulary wrong loses credibility immediately.

## What case interviews actually test

Not framework recitation. Real screening dimensions:

1. **Structure.** Can you decompose a vague business question into a small number of MECE drivers? Not a checklist — actual problem-solving.
2. **Quantitative reasoning.** Can you do mental math under pressure without losing the thread of the case? Multiplication of large numbers, percentage and fraction manipulation, breakeven calculation.
3. **Insight.** Can you find the non-obvious driver? What makes this business different from the surface read?
4. **Communication.** Are your answers crisp? Can you reach a "so what" conclusion that a partner could read in 30 seconds?
5. **Pressure response.** Do you stay calm when the interviewer pushes back? Do you adjust when new data arrives?

A common WSG-reader mistake: treating the case as a memorized framework dump. The interviewer wants to see how you think, not which framework you've memorized.

## The five canonical case types

Every case at MBB, tier-2, and most Big 4 strategy interviews maps to one of five types. Get the type right first; structure follows.

### 1. Profitability case

**Question form:** "Our client is a [X] business and profits are declining. Why?"

**Canonical structure:**
- Profit = Revenue − Cost
- Revenue = Price × Volume
- Volume = Number of customers × Quantity per customer
- Cost = Fixed cost + Variable cost (or COGS + SG&A + other)

**What to actually probe:**
- Is the decline a revenue problem, a cost problem, or both?
- On revenue: price, volume, mix?
- On cost: which cost line is moving?
- External factors: macro, competitive, regulatory?

**Signature mistake:** Diving straight into "what to do about it" before isolating the cause. Always isolate first.

### 2. Market entry case

**Question form:** "Should our client enter [new market / new geography / new product line]?"

**Canonical structure:**
- Market attractiveness — size, growth rate, competitive intensity, customer behavior
- Client capability fit — can we win? Do we have the right assets, brand, distribution?
- Financial case — investment required, revenue forecast, time to breakeven, NPV
- Risks and exit options

**What to actually probe:**
- Use a customer / competitor / company / context (4Cs) frame to start, then drill into the financials.
- Confirm what "win" means before answering whether the client can win.

**Signature mistake:** Recommending entry without naming the specific go-to-market mechanism. Always specify channel, pricing, partnerships.

### 3. M&A / acquisition case

**Question form:** "Should our client acquire Target X?"

**Canonical structure:**
- Strategic rationale — why this target?
- Synergies — revenue (cross-sell, geographic) and cost (consolidation, scale)
- Standalone value of target — fair multiple, fair valuation
- Integration risk — cultural fit, IT/ops, customer overlap
- Alternative uses of capital — buyback, organic investment, alternative targets

**What to actually probe:**
- Is this a growth, cost, or capability acquisition?
- What's the bid premium and is it justified?
- What's the integration timeline and risk?

**Signature mistake:** Skipping the "alternatives" question. Always ask what else the client could do with the same capital.

### 4. Operations / cost reduction case

**Question form:** "Our client's operating margins are below industry average. Where can we cut?"

**Canonical structure:**
- Value chain decomposition — inbound logistics, operations, outbound, sales/marketing, service
- Cost driver analysis — what's the biggest cost line and is it benchmark-able?
- Process inefficiency — where in the chain do we lose dollars unnecessarily?
- People / org — span of control, role redundancy, automation opportunity

**What to actually probe:**
- Where does the cost sit (which function / which step)?
- Is the cost structurally necessary or a result of inefficiency?
- What's the implementation risk of any cut?

**Signature mistake:** Recommending layoffs without naming the operational fix that enables them.

### 5. Market sizing / estimation

**Question form:** "How many gas stations are there in the US?" or "What's the annual market for diapers in Brazil?"

**Canonical structure:**
- Top-down — start with a known population, ratio down to the target metric.
- Bottom-up — start with a unit and build up.
- Pick whichever the data supports best. Make assumptions explicit. Sanity-check the final number.

**What to actually probe:**
- State your assumptions clearly. Interviewer wants to see the math, not the right answer.
- Cross-check against a second method if time permits.

**Signature mistake:** Getting hung up on precise numbers. The framework and reasoning matter more than the actual answer.

## The 4Cs / 4Ps and when they actually help

**4Cs (Customer, Competitor, Company, Context)** — useful at the start of a market-entry or growth-strategy case to ensure breadth. Not a framework to "apply." Just a checklist.

**4Ps (Product, Price, Place, Promotion)** — useful inside a market entry or revenue-strategy case when diving into the go-to-market.

**Porter's Five Forces** — useful for industry analysis. Often asked at McKinsey when the case has a clear industry-structure dimension.

**SWOT** — almost never useful in a case interview. Avoid.

**BCG Matrix / GE Matrix** — almost never useful at undergrad-analyst level. Reserve for portfolio strategy cases at the MBA level.

## What WSG articles should and should not say

**Should say:**
- "Build your own structure for the specific case. Avoid memorized frameworks."
- "The five canonical case types are profitability, market entry, M&A, operations, and market sizing."
- "MBB cases are structured around drivers, not frameworks."
- "Quantitative pressure is real — practice mental math separately."

**Should not say:**
- "Use Porter's Five Forces for every case." (False.)
- "There are 20 case-interview frameworks you need to memorize." (False — and counterproductive.)
- "The interviewer wants you to recite a framework." (Wrong — they want to see your thinking.)
- "Case Interview Secrets by Victor Cheng is enough." (One of several resources; not sufficient alone.)

## Resources to reference in articles

- **Case in Point** by Marc Cosentino — the most-cited entry-level book.
- **Case Interview Secrets** by Victor Cheng — strong on framework-free thinking; some content is outdated.
- **Firm-published case-prep materials** — McKinsey, BCG, Bain, Deloitte, Oliver Wyman, L.E.K. all publish official case-prep guides. WSG articles should link to these as the primary source.
- **IGotAnOffer** — strong case-walkthroughs at igotanoffer.com/blogs/mckinsey-case-interview-blog/case-interview-examples.
- **PrepLounge** — partner-practice platform. Heavy community-case library at preplounge.com/en/management-consulting-cases.
- **Crafting Cases** — case-walkthrough video resource at craftingcases.com/case-interview-examples.
- **Hacking the Case Interview** — practitioner-written content at hackingthecaseinterview.com.

WSG articles on case interviews should NEVER recommend paid third-party programs without explicit disclosure of the price and a real assessment of whether it's worth the cost. The firm-published resources are free.

## How to write case-interview claims in articles

**Always name the case type** when describing a problem. "Profitability case" or "market-entry case" — not "business case."

**Always link to the firm's own case prep page** when discussing firm-specific cases. McKinsey's, BCG's, and Bain's case-prep pages are evergreen and authoritative.

**Never invent a "framework."** If the article needs to introduce a framework, use one of the canonical ones above.

**Cite the resources by name, not generic.** "Case in Point by Marc Cosentino" — not "a popular case interview book."
