# WSG Writing Style Guide

This is the source-of-truth style guide. The `wsg-article` skill loads this file on every article generation. Update this file when your voice or rules evolve, and the skill picks up the changes automatically.

---

## Who we write for

American undergraduates (freshman to junior) who are driven and slightly anxious about recruiting. They are smart and impatient. If the writing feels generic, padded, or condescending, they will leave. Write like you are speaking to a sharp peer.

## The goal of every post

Every blog post should do three things:

1. Answer the keyword question clearly and honestly
2. Make the reader feel understood and that their time was well spent
3. Establish credibility through real experience

**The most important principle:** the post should be valuable even if the reader never becomes a customer. If someone reads a WSG post and feels like they just got 15 minutes of real advice from someone in the industry, that is a win.

Ask: if this showed up when you were a sophomore trying to break into IB, would you be glad you clicked it?

## Establishing credibility

What sets a WSG post apart is real experience. But having it is not enough. It needs to show on the page.

1. **Reference something specific** — not "I have worked with many students," but "I had a student who networked with 60 bankers and still did not get an offer. The issue was not outreach. It was his story."
2. **Name the moment, not just the lesson** — instead of "networking is important," write "the summer before my junior year I sent 40 cold emails and got three responses. Those led to two referrals. One became an offer."
3. **Admit what surprised you or what you got wrong.** Credibility comes from having done the work, not from being perfect.

**The test:** Is there anything here that only someone who has actually done this would know? If not, the experience is not on the page yet.

## Writing DOS

1. **Write like you are talking to one specific person.** Picture a sophomore at a large state school who just googled this at 11pm. Use "you."
   - Good: "You don't need a finance degree to get into Goldman Sachs. But you do need to understand why you want to be there, and most candidates who apply can't answer that question clearly."
   - Bad: "Many students are unsure about the requirements for investment banking roles."

2. **Start with something real, not a definition.** Open with tension, a sharp observation, or a direct statement.
   - Good: "Let's skip the part where I tell you investment banking is 'very competitive.' You already know that. You're here because you want to know what actually determines who gets in."
   - Bad: "Investment banking is one of the most sought-after career paths for college students today..."

3. **Lead with the most useful information early.** Short intro → list starts → substance immediately. Do not make the reader scroll for value.

4. **Make every list item genuinely useful.**
   - Good: "**Network before applications open, not after.** Most students start reaching out when applications go live. By then, candidates with relationships are already ahead. Start the summer before junior year. Aim for 30 meaningful conversations before applying."
   - Bad: "**Networking is important.** Investment banking is a relationship-driven industry..."

5. **Use specific numbers, timelines, and examples.**
   - Good: "Many banks open applications in October of your sophomore year for internships that start 20 months later."
   - Bad: "Banks recruit early..."

6. **Write short sentences for key points.**
   - Good: "A generic story is instantly forgettable. Forgettable candidates don't get offers."

7. **Sound like someone who has been through it.** Use first-person experience, named situations, real numbers.

8. **Bold full sentences that carry insight.** Not random words — full sentences that the reader could pull as a quote.
   - Good: **"A 3.3 GPA with a strong network will outperform a 3.8 GPA with no relationships."**

9. **End with a clear, natural CTA.** No need to plug WSG at the end unless it can be done flawlessly. The main focus is to create strong, high quality articles that don't read like AI. Readers will naturally be interested in WSG as a result.

## Writing DON'TS

1. **Don't use filler phrases.** Cut these and replace:

   | Cut this | Replace with |
   | --- | --- |
   | "Investment banking is highly competitive" | Explain what makes it competitive |
   | "It's important to note that..." | Just say it |
   | "In today's landscape..." | Delete |
   | "Many students find..." | Be specific |

2. **Don't write empty list items.** Every item explains how, when, and what outcome to expect.

3. **Don't pad word count.** If you can cut it without losing meaning, cut it.

4. **Don't explain basics.** No definitions of investment banking. This is not Wikipedia.

5. **Don't give advice you wouldn't give a friend.** No generic motivation.

6. **Don't use dashes or hyphens as structural punctuation.**
   - Bad: "Networking is important — especially early..."
   - Good: "Networking is especially important early. Referrals change everything."

## Post structure (every post follows this)

### Title
Use the exact headline from the content calendar. Do not change it.

### Meta description
1 to 2 sentences, 150 to 160 characters, including the keyword naturally. Write separately from the post.

### Introduction
Two jobs:

1. Make the reader feel understood
2. Give them a reason to keep reading

A strong intro reflects the reader's situation, creates tension, and resolves it quickly. It should end with a clear promise of value.

**Example (from "How to Break Into Investment Banking"):**

> Most students assume breaking into investment banking requires a finance degree, a target school, and a father who golfs with a Goldman partner. None of that is true. Every year, students from large state schools, non-finance majors, and first-generation backgrounds land offers at Goldman Sachs, Evercore, and Morgan Stanley. They do it by understanding a process most of their peers never figure out. This guide breaks that process down into ten concrete steps, starting from wherever you are right now.

**Why this works:** Opens with the reader's assumption, not a definition. Skips basic explanations. Creates tension, then resolves it. Ends with a clear promise.

### H2 sub-questions (required)
After the intro, include 1 to 2 H2 questions that reflect real search queries. Answer each in 2 to 4 sentences. These help with SEO, AI summaries, and featured snippets.

**Example:**

> **How hard is it to break into investment banking, realistically?**
> Very hard, but not randomly hard. At top firms, acceptance rates for summer analyst roles are often under 2 to 3 percent. Success depends less on raw intelligence and more on executing a specific process across key areas like networking, story, and timing.
>
> **Do you need a finance degree to get into investment banking?**
> No. Banks hire across majors. What matters is your story, your ability to pass technical interviews, and the effort you put into building relationships within firms.

### Body
Build around the brief. See the format-specific templates for body structure (ultimate-guide, listicle, niche, deep-dive).

### Conclusion (1 to 2 paragraphs)
Do not repeat the post. Push the reader to the next step: a decision, a shift in thinking, or a clear action.

## Format-specific guidance

### Listicles (resources)
- Stick to the number in the title
- Do not just list links
- Explain what, why, and how
- Add your perspective on each item
- Make each item actionable
- Hyperlink the anchor word inline (e.g., "[Mergers & Inquisitions](url)") rather than listing URLs below

### Insight-led / ultimate guides
- Each section is an insight, not a topic
- Strong, opinionated headings
- Back claims with experience
- Tie every insight to action
- Maintain a clear argument throughout
- Do not summarize — assert

### Reviews
- Be honest, especially about cons
- Clear structure: what it is, pros, cons, who it is for
- Keep section formats consistent across reviews
- Focus on who should and shouldn't use it
- Earn the recommendation

### Niche (firm/role/edge-case)
- Pull specifics from the relevant deep-dive in `deep-dives/`
- Anchor in real names, deals, or scenarios where possible
- Frame around the specific reader (e.g., "junior at a non-target eyeing RX")
- Don't waste paragraphs explaining the topic at a 101 level — that's what the deep-dive is for

## Tone reference

After every draft, ask:

- Does this sound like real experience?
- Would a smart junior find this useful?
- Could another blog have written this?
- If I cut this, would it matter?

If it sounds generic, rewrite it.

## Brief mechanics

- **Target keyword:** use early, in subheadings, and naturally throughout
- **Headline:** fixed (use exactly what's in the content calendar)
- **Word count:** 1,800 to 2,500 words. No padding.
- **H2 sub-questions:** required
- **Hyperlinks:** link sources and tools inline as anchor words

## A note on AI

AI is fine for research and rough drafts. It is not fine to ship.

Rewrite everything in the WSG voice. Add real insight and experience. If there is no sentence that only someone who has been through this could have written, it is not ready.

## The AI sniff test (catch the cadence)

Even when an AI draft passes the no-em-dashes, no-filler-phrases checklist, it can still read like AI. The tells are subtle and a reader who has read 50 ChatGPT articles can usually pick it up within a paragraph. Watch for these patterns and break them during editing.

### The three-beat rhythm

AI defaults to a predictable three-beat paragraph: setup sentence, evidence sentence, bolded insight sentence. Every section. **The fix is to break the pattern in at least three places per article.** Drop a one-sentence paragraph. Let a section end without a bolded line. Open one section with a question instead of a statement. The reader's pattern-detector relaxes when the rhythm stops being predictable.

### Symmetric paragraph length

AI tends to produce paragraphs that are all roughly the same length, usually 60-90 words. Real writing has more variance. **Some paragraphs should be one sentence. Some should be 150 words.** Look at your draft and ask: if I covered the bolded sentences, would the paragraph shapes alone betray that this was AI? If yes, vary the lengths.

### Over-clean transitions

AI writes very clean transitions: "But you are not out of options." "Here is the honest situation." "The reason most students sleep on this is..." These read smoothly but they all sound like an AI signaling a transition. **Drop transitions sometimes. Just start the next idea.** It feels rougher, but it reads more like a human who actually has the thought.

### The "you" overload

AI loves second-person address. Every other sentence becomes "you should," "if you are," "you will find." Real WSG writers slip in and out of second person depending on what they are arguing. **Replace one in three "you" instances with first person, third person, or a direct claim.** "You should network before applications open" becomes "Networking before applications open is what separates the offers from the rejections."

### Named specifics that are not actually named

This is the trap. AI writes things like "I had a student who networked aggressively and got an offer." That looks specific. It is not. A real specific has a name (or initials), a school, a firm, a number, a date. **Every credibility moment should pass this test: could a reader nod and say "ok, I believe that happened to a real person"?** If the answer is no, the moment is theater. Either make it real or cut it.

### The catch-all check

After editing, read three random paragraphs aloud. If they sound like the same person wrote them with the same enthusiasm level, in the same rhythm, the AI is still showing. Real writing varies in intensity. Some paragraphs are flat reporting. Some are punchy. Some are reflective. **An article where every paragraph hits the same emotional beat is an AI tell, even if every word is technically yours now.**
