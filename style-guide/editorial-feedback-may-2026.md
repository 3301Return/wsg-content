# Editorial Feedback Log (May 2026, Stephen)

Captured from review of the first three Claude-generated articles. These are now part of the WSG voice. The skill and main style guide have been updated to enforce them.

## 1. Listicle item structure (HARD RULE)

Every listicle item must be **scannable in bullet form**, not chunky prose. For program/firm/resource roundups, each item gets:

- **Website / Link**
- **Where to apply / How to apply**
- **Application deadline** (or window)
- **Eligibility criteria** (A, B, C, D)
- **Who it's for / Why apply** (one or two sentences)
- A short description after the bullets — not a strategy essay

Right now Claude drafts these as chunky paragraphs. Stephen flagged this on both the sophomore-insights article and the internships article. **Skimming is the core thing readers do; bullets enable skimming; chunky paragraphs prevent it.**

## 2. Bolding rule (HARD RULE)

A bolded sentence must be **readable independently and convey the main perspective of its paragraph or the article.**

Bad: bolding "It is not" (no meaning standalone)
Bad: "If you are a sophomore, the highest-leverage move is the third path, not the first." (reader has no idea what "third path" is)

Good: "Sophomore and freshman insight programs are the front door to junior summer recruiting at top firms."
Good: "The best insights programs are accelerated pipelines where the firm scores you for a real offer."

Test: read only the bolded sentences in order. Can a reader get the article's argument? If no, the bolding is wrong.

## 3. Drop "Honest Read" / stock sub-headers when they don't aid skimming

Stephen flagged the "Honest Read:" sub-header in the sophomore article as not useful for scanning. Replace with description-of-program content (what it is, who it's looking for, who is competitive). Same caution applies to any stock label that adds bulk without information density.

## 4. Frame positively, not as a reverse framing

Bad: "Most candidates over-prepare for technicals and under-prepare for behaviorals."
Good: "Behaviorals are one of the most important factors in offer decisions. Technicals are easy to practice for and binary; behaviorals carry more weight in who ultimately gets picked."

Lead with what is important. Do not lead with what is overrated.

## 5. Add "company perspective" framing

For articles about programs, firms, or any recruiting process, **start with the bank/company perspective**: what is the company trying to achieve through this program? What candidate type does this serve their goals? Then back-derive how a candidate competes for it.

Stephen: "I think for everything we need to do a good job of taking the perspective of the company first."

## 6. Add concrete "say this / don't say that" examples

For behavioral-style or how-to-answer pieces, **include direct examples at the end**. Format:

> **Why this firm?**
> Don't say: "Goldman Sachs is very prestigious."
> Say: "I spoke to Sarah in your healthcare group last month and she described [specific]."

These callout examples are more useful than abstract structural advice alone.

## 7. Tighten intros (HARD RULE)

Intros should lead with the main point in 1-2 short paragraphs. Big four-paragraph buildups are slow.

For listicles, the opening should answer: what is this list, why does it matter, with a quantifiable claim if possible.

Example for sophomore insights: "Insight Programs are one of the best ways to get into Bulge Bracket and Elite Boutique firms in investment banking. Students who complete these programs are 5-10x more likely to get an offer than those who don't."

## 8. Use contractions and flow-y language

"don't" not "do not". "you're" not "you are". "it's" not "it is" (except where it would be misread). Reads more like real speech.

Stephen: "I would try to feed into AI usage of more flow-y words like don't vs. do not."

## 9. Paragraph spacing

Use clear blank-line spacing between paragraphs for scannability. Walls of text fail the skim test.

## 10a. Address the top firms that aren't on the list (May 23 review)

For any "Top N" list where the most-discussed firms in the category don't actually recruit through the channels being listed, **the article must include a short section explaining how readers should approach those firms**. The reader will notice the gap and bounce if it isn't addressed.

Examples:
- A "Top 10 VC Internships" list should briefly explain that a16z, Sequoia, and Benchmark don't recruit undergrads directly, and the realistic path is to break in at a less-prestigious VC after these programs and then network sideways into the top firms.
- A "Top 10 PE Recruiting Firms (headhunters)" list should note that the megafunds (Blackstone, KKR, Apollo) maintain direct campus recruiting teams alongside the headhunters, so for direct sophomore recruiting the headhunter route is less direct.
- A "Top 10 Sophomore Insights Programs" list should note that Centerview's sophomore programming is invite-only and not on the listed application channel.

Format: one short section near the end of the list with a 2-3 sentence treatment per firm or category. Title it something like "How to get into the top firms not on this list" or "The exception list."

## 10b. Hard paragraph breaks at logical pivots (May 23 review)

Within a section, **insert a blank line whenever the argument pivots between two distinct ideas**. This is more aggressive than just paragraph spacing between sections.

Pattern to break:

> The on-cycle direct path is right for: target-school students with 3.7+ GPA and prior finance experience, students with existing PE networks, students fluent in modeling before sophomore summer. The IB-first path is right for: students who didn't engage until junior year, students who want optionality across PE/HF/CorpDev, students who want the deal-team training and brand of an IB analyst stint.

The right form:

> The on-cycle direct path is right for: target-school students with 3.7+ GPA and prior finance experience, students with existing PE networks, students fluent in modeling before sophomore summer.
>
> The IB-first path is right for: students who didn't engage until junior year, students who want optionality across PE/HF/CorpDev, students who want the deal-team training and brand of an IB analyst stint.

The rule: if a sentence introduces a new "this is right for" / "the other side is" / "alternatively" pivot, that sentence starts a new paragraph. No exceptions.

## 10c. Niche-specific freshness check (May 23 review)

Before finalizing any curated list, ask: **is there a hot or emergent firm/program in a specific sub-niche that should be on this list?** The standard 10 firms often miss the newest entrant that's actually best-in-class for a sliver of the audience.

Examples flagged by reviewers in past articles:
- VC for technical undergrads: NEO Capital is widely considered the strongest fund to be associated with in 2026 for technically-strong college students.
- Restructuring: Centerview is breaking into RX competitively in 2026.

Before submitting any "Top N" list, do one quick web search of "best [niche] firm/program for [specific student profile] 2026" to confirm the list is current. If a clearly-better-than-listed firm exists for a real sub-niche, swap it in or add an honorable mention.

## 10d. Don't recite the timeline math at the reader (May 23 second review)

The `style-guide/recruiting-timeline-glossary.md` exists so I do the math correctly. It is not content for the article. **Don't insert paragraphs like "the students applying are sophomores in the 2026-2027 academic year and will graduate Spring 2029."** The reader already knows they're a sophomore. The headline gives the cycle year. The eligibility lines under each firm give the graduation year. That is sufficient.

If the headline says "to Apply To in 2027" and the first eligibility line says "Sophomores graduating December 2028 to June 2029," the math is conveyed. Pedantically restating it in the intro is condescending and pads the word count.

When in doubt: would a sophomore applying right now find this sentence useful, or would they already know it because they're the audience? If it's the latter, cut it.

## 10e. RX (Restructuring) is an Elite Boutique product, not a Bulge Bracket coverage group (May 23 second review)

In any BB section, the group preferences to name are coverage and product groups that BBs are known for: TMT, healthcare, FIG, industrials, consumer & retail, energy, real estate, M&A, leveraged finance, and ECM/DCM. **Do not list RX (or "Restructuring") as a BB group preference.** Goldman has RSSG, JPMorgan has a Special Situations Group, but they are small relative to the BB coverage tree and aren't what most candidates target.

RX is structurally an Elite Boutique and boutique-bank product. The firms to name when discussing RX:
- Houlihan Lokey Financial Restructuring (the deepest practice by deal count)
- PJT Partners Restructuring and Special Situations Group
- Lazard Restructuring
- Moelis Restructuring
- Evercore Restructuring
- Greenhill Restructuring

If an article mentions RX, the firm context must be one of those, not a BB.

## 10f. Capitalize "Investment Banking Summer Analyst" as a defined role title (May 23 second review)

When referring to the role title or the program as a defined noun phrase, capitalize each word: **"Investment Banking Summer Analyst"** or **"Summer Analyst Program"**.

Capitalize:
- "Investment Banking Summer Analyst"
- "Summer Analyst" (when standing in for the role)
- "Summer Analyst Program"
- "Junior Summer Analyst seat"
- "Sophomore Summer Analyst Program"
- "Global Investment Banking Summer Analyst Program"

Lowercase is acceptable only when used purely descriptively, not as the role title. Examples:
- "a summer banking internship" (generic descriptor of an activity)
- "the summer analyst experience" (descriptor, not title)
- "investment banking" as a field reference (lowercase is conventional)

If you're naming a specific program, role, or seat type, capitalize. If you're describing the work generically, lowercase is fine.

## 11. Time horizon

For internships and any recruiting article, **target the next open cycle, not the one that just closed**. SEO rankings take 2-3 months. An article about Summer 2026 internships written in May 2026 is essentially obsolete by ranking time.

Default: write for the cycle that opens 6+ months from publication date.

## 12. Headline rules (May 25 update — applies to ALL articles)

Captured from the consulting keyword PDF. These are universal headline rules, not consulting-specific.

### 12a. Keep headlines under 8-10 words

Long headlines lose ranking in Google SERPs and lose clicks in OG cards. Aim for 6-9 words.

Bad: "The Ultimate Guide to the MECE Framework: How to Solve Cases Like a McKinsey Consultant" (16 words)

Good: "Ultimate Guide to the MECE Framework" (6 words)

### 12b. Lead with opportunity or value, not abstract opinion

The headline must answer either "What set of opportunities does this give me?" OR "How do I get to a specific outcome?" If it does neither, rewrite.

Bad: "Why You Shouldn't Overlook Boutique Consulting Firms (And How to Land an Offer)" — abstract, no opportunity signal.

Good: "10 Boutique Consulting Firms to Apply To" — names a clear opportunity set.

Good: "Ultimate Guide to Get Into Boutique Consulting" — names the value (the how).

### 12c. Avoid college-essay framing

Drop colon-phrases that read like academic papers ("Beyond the Case:", "From Panic to Passed:", "Cracking the Code:", "The Insider Blueprint:", "Stepping Stones:", "The Rise of...").

Bad: "Beyond the Case: How to Master Consulting Fit Interview Questions"

Good: "How to Master Consulting Fit Interview Questions"

Bad: "From Panic to Passed: How to Prepare for Your First Case Study Interview"

Good: "How to Prepare for Your First Case Interview"

### 12d. Describe the value quickly

Headline + meta description's first line together should tell the reader exactly what they get. Never make them guess.
