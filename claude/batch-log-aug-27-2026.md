## Revision rounds (same day, founder feedback) — STANDING EDITORIAL RULES

Two rounds of feedback on this batch produced rules that should govern every future batch. **Consider folding all of these into `style-guide/editorial-rules.md` and the templates.**

### Round 1: intros

1. **Every article opens with a hook plus the Stephen Turban intro** ("My name is Stephen Turban..." / "I'm Stephen Turban, founder of WSG..."). Vary the structure article to article; don't run one template. Hooks don't have to be student stories: an inbox pattern, a scene, a first-call quote, a decision moment all work. Composite people get single-letter names (this batch: M, R, T, K, S, N, P, D, J). Intros stay tight (2-3 short paragraphs) with the target keyword in the first 100 words.
2. **Line-edit for strained/overpacked sentences.** One idea per clause; don't chain three ideas into one bolded sentence.

### Round 2: story polarity and diction

3. **WSG never looks bad in its own stories.** A story where "a student I coached through WSG" fails makes WSG look ineffective. The fix used across this batch: setbacks happen BEFORE someone finds WSG ("came to WSG a week after her Bain rejection..."), and students advised by WSG win (offers, successful reroutes, good decisions between two offers). Hooks can also be a roommate/friend/non-student frame — but don't invent new biography for Stephen beyond the writer profile.
4. **No implausible effort counts in stories.** "Ran 42 practice cases," "two hundred flashcards," "40 hours of Moyer, 20 of Kricheff, 60 of waterfall reps" read as fake precision. Use plausible, roundable language ("a semester's worth of flashcards," "a fall working through Moyer and Kricheff"). Real outcome numbers (a $9,000 sponsorship close, a 3.6 GPA) stay.
5. **Cut copywriter-isms and off-register phrases.** Flagged and removed this batch: "That's the headline," "the caveats the recruiting brochures leave out," "before counting retirement money," "life happens in deposits," "Rent is not national," "which kind of tired you want to be," "the smallest interesting number," "reads as a future engagement summary," "the interview's real center of gravity," "in every way that counts," "an embarrassment of material," "no faking automatic," "choosing on noise," "holdouts need dragging," "case clock," "cheap homework," "mental math under a clock," "pressure-tested for your fingerprint." The test: would Stephen say this sentence out loud to a student? If not, write the plain version.
